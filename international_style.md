# International Style

## What it is

The International Style, also called the International Typographic Style or Swiss Style, became especially influential in Switzerland during the 1950s and 1960s. It developed from earlier modernist ideas and focused on clear communication, order, and simplicity. The style brought together ideas from the Zurich and Basel schools of graphic design and became influential internationally.

The style is connected to Modernism because it emphasizes function, structure, and clarity instead of unnecessary decoration. Designers used typography, grids, photography, and geometric organization to make information easier to understand.

## Recognize and apply it

Three visual features that can help identify the International Style are:

1. **Grid-based layouts** — A typographic grid organizes text and other visual elements into a clear structure.
2. **Sans-serif typography** — Clean sans-serif typefaces are commonly used for straightforward and readable communication.
3. **Minimal and structured composition** — Designs often use asymmetry, limited decoration, photography, and carefully organized spacing.

The style works well when information needs to be clear, organized, and easy to read. These features are especially useful for posters, websites, information displays, and other designs where communication is the main goal.

## Historical examples

### Example 1: Musica Viva, 1957

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Musica Viva*, 1957, Museum of Modern Art (MoMA).
- **Direct collection/source link:** [MoMA — Musica Viva, 1957](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4885)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View the work at MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4885). Credit: Gift of the artist. MoMA directs users seeking to reproduce artwork images to its licensing services.
- **What I observe:** The poster uses geometric forms, organized spacing, and typography in a controlled composition. The elements are arranged in a structured way rather than being randomly placed.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would use a simple grid to organize the headline, image, supporting text, and CTA so the most important information is easy to find.

### Example 2: The Family of Man, 1957–58

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** Josef Müller-Brockmann, *The Family of Man*, 1957–58, Museum of Modern Art (MoMA).
- **Direct collection/source link:** [MoMA — The Family of Man, 1957–58](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/8806)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View the work at MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/8806). Credit: Gift of the designer. Follow MoMA's licensing guidance before reproducing the image.
- **What I observe:** The design uses geometric organization, strong visual structure, typography, and carefully arranged photographic elements. The composition creates order while still making the main visual elements noticeable.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would use a structured layout and carefully controlled image placement to make the main product message stand out without adding unnecessary decoration.
