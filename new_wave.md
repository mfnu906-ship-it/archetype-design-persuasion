# New Wave

[Home](README.md)

## What it is

New Wave is a postmodern graphic design style that developed from experiments with Swiss typography beginning in the late 1960s and 1970s. Designer Wolfgang Weingart helped develop the style by challenging the strict rules, grids, and organization associated with traditional Swiss design. His work influenced other designers, including April Greiman, who helped expand New Wave design in the United States.

Instead of always focusing on order and maximum readability, New Wave designers experimented with typography, spacing, layering, and unconventional layouts. The style challenged traditional expectations of graphic design and allowed typography and other visual elements to become more expressive.

## Recognize and apply it

Three visual features that can help identify New Wave are:

1. **Experimental typography** — Text can change in size, direction, spacing, and position instead of following a consistent typographic structure.
2. **Layering and overlapping elements** — Text, images, shapes, and textures can overlap to create visual complexity and depth.
3. **Irregular and diagonal layouts** — Elements may break away from a traditional grid through diagonal lines, unexpected placement, and asymmetrical compositions.

The style works well when a design needs to feel expressive, energetic, unconventional, or experimental. These features can be applied to posters and website hero sections through expressive typography, layered imagery, irregular placement, and compositions that intentionally challenge a traditional grid.

## Historical examples

### Example 1: Typographic Process, Nr 4. Typographic Signs, 1971–72

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** Wolfgang Weingart, *Typographic Process, Nr 4. Typographic Signs*, 1971–72, Museum of Modern Art (MoMA).
- **Direct collection/source link:** [MoMA — Typographic Process, Nr 4. Typographic Signs](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/86506)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View the work at MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/86506). Credit: Jürg Zumtobel Purchase Fund. Follow MoMA's licensing guidance before reproducing the image.
- **What I observe:** The design treats letters and typographic forms as visual elements rather than simply arranging text for easy reading. The different forms, spacing, and arrangements create a more experimental composition than a traditional grid-based design.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would experiment with the size, spacing, and placement of the headline instead of keeping all of the typography aligned to a strict grid.

### Example 2: Design Quarterly issue 133 poster, 1986

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** April Greiman, *Design Quarterly issue 133 poster*, 1986, San Francisco Museum of Modern Art (SFMOMA).
- **Direct collection/source link:** [SFMOMA — Design Quarterly issue 133 poster](https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/96.366)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View the work at SFMOMA](https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/96.366). Credit: Collection SFMOMA, Accessions Committee Fund purchase. Follow SFMOMA's terms before reproducing the image.
- **What I observe:** The design combines imagery, typography, and digital elements in a layered composition. Instead of following a simple structured layout, the different visual elements interact and overlap, creating a more experimental and expressive design.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would layer images, typography, shapes, and textures while using irregular placement to make the hero feel more creative and visually unexpected.
