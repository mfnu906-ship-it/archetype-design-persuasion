# Deconstructivism

[Home](README.md)

## What it is

Deconstructivism is an experimental approach to design that became influential in the 1980s and 1990s. Instead of organizing information through a simple and predictable structure, designers experimented with fragmentation, layering, overlapping elements, and unconventional arrangements of text and images.

The style challenged many Modernist ideas about clarity, order, and strict visual organization. Rather than always making typography and images follow a clean grid, Deconstructivist design could break apart traditional layouts and make viewers look more carefully at how text and images communicate.

## Recognize and apply it

Three visual features that can help identify Deconstructivism are:

1. **Fragmented layouts** — Text and images may be separated, interrupted, or arranged in unexpected positions instead of following a simple grid.
2. **Layering and overlapping** — Typography, photographs, and graphic elements can overlap to create visual complexity and multiple levels of information.
3. **Experimental typography** — Words may use different sizes, directions, spacing, or placements so typography becomes part of the visual composition.

The style works well when a design should feel experimental, expressive, or visually challenging. In a hero design, these features could create visual interest while the main headline and CTA remain readable.

## Historical examples

### Example 1: The Graduate Program in Design, 1989

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** Katherine McCoy, *The Graduate Program in Design*, 1989, Cranbrook Art Museum.
- **Direct collection/source link:** [Cranbrook Art Museum — The Graduate Program in Design](https://cranbrookartmuseum.org/artwork/katherine-mccoy-posters-the-graduate-program-in-design/)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View the work at Cranbrook Art Museum](https://cranbrookartmuseum.org/artwork/katherine-mccoy-posters-the-graduate-program-in-design/). Credit: Gift of Katherine and Michael McCoy. Follow the museum's guidance before reproducing the image.
- **What I observe:** The poster combines different words, images, colors, and directions in a layered composition. Text and images interact with each other instead of being separated into a simple traditional layout.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would layer selected text and images and use different alignments to create visual movement while keeping the main headline and CTA easy to identify.

### Example 2: Cranbrook Architecture 1980/81

- **Creator, title, date, institution:** Katherine McCoy, *Cranbrook Architecture 1980/81, Masters Program*, ca. 1980, Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum.
- **Direct collection/source link:** [Smithsonian — Cranbrook Architecture 1980/81](https://www.si.edu/object/cranbrook-architecture-198081-masters-program-cranbrook-academy-art-bloomfield-hills-mi%3Achndm_1995-162-18)
- **Image or viewing link; credit and reuse terms:** [View the work at the Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/object/cranbrook-architecture-198081-masters-program-cranbrook-academy-art-bloomfield-hills-mi%3Achndm_1995-162-18). Credit: Gift of Katherine McCoy. Smithsonian states that usage conditions apply to reuse of the image.
- **What I observe:** The poster integrates text, photographs, architectural models, and floor-plan imagery. Some visual elements are superimposed over text, creating a layered composition rather than keeping every element separate.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** I would overlap selected imagery with supporting text and use an unconventional arrangement to create depth while leaving enough clear space around the CTA.
